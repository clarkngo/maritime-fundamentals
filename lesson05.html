Cargo Operations

Loading a ship correctly is a planning problem as much as a physical one — get the sequence, weight distribution, or paperwork wrong, and the consequences range from a delayed sailing to a genuine stability hazard.

Lesson Overview

A ship doesn't just get "loaded" the way a truck gets loaded — where and in what order cargo goes aboard is the product of careful planning, because a vessel's stability, structural stress, and even the order in which cargo can be discharged at later ports all depend on decisions made before a single container or tonne of bulk cargo crosses the rail. This lesson covers that planning process, the special handling dangerous goods require, the paperwork that legally represents cargo in transit, and a few of the special cargo types that complicate an otherwise routine loading.

As with the rest of this course, the goal is literacy, not qualification: you should finish this lesson able to explain why a stowage plan exists and what a bill of lading actually does, without needing to draw one yourself.

Anchoring analogy

Loading a ship is closer to packing a moving truck for a multi-stop delivery route than to simply filling a container: you have to think about weight distribution (so the truck doesn't tip), about what needs to come out first (so it goes in last), and about which items can't be packed next to each other (so a leaking can doesn't ruin the boxes next to it). Cargo operations do all of this at a much larger scale, with legal paperwork attached to every item.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe the basic sequence of loading and discharging cargo at a port call.
  • Explain what a stowage plan is and why weight distribution and stability matter when loading a ship.
  • Describe what the IMDG Code covers and why dangerous goods require special handling and segregation.
  • Explain what a bill of lading is and its three core functions.
  • Identify common special cargo considerations, including heavy-lift, refrigerated, and deck cargo.

The Loading & Discharging Sequence

≈ 25 min

Before a ship even arrives at berth, its officers and the terminal's cargo planners exchange a proposed cargo plan detailing what will load or discharge, in what sequence, and where each item will sit aboard. Once alongside, hatches (or, for a container ship, cell guides) are opened, cranes or ramps begin working according to that plan, and a tally clerk counts and records each unit of cargo crossing the ship's rail — a running physical count that must match the paperwork described later in this lesson.

Discharging follows a mirror-image logic: cargo due off at the current port must be accessible without needing to move cargo destined for a later port out of the way first — which is exactly why the stowage plan in Section 2 has to account for a ship's entire multi-port itinerary, not just the next call.

Stowage Planning & Basic Stability

≈ 40 min

A stowage plan (sometimes called a bay plan on a container ship) is a detailed diagram showing exactly where every unit of cargo sits aboard the vessel — which hold, which tier, which position. It exists to solve several problems simultaneously: distributing weight so the ship remains stable and its hull isn't overstressed, keeping incompatible cargoes apart, and — on a multi-port voyage — sequencing cargo so nothing bound for a later port blocks access to cargo due off sooner.

Weight distribution affects a vessel's stability (its tendency to return upright after rolling) and its structural stress (how much bending force the hull itself experiences). Cargo loaded too high raises the ship's center of gravity and can make it dangerously "tender" (slow and excessive to roll); cargo loaded unevenly along the hull's length can overstress the structure. Getting this right is why stowage planning is treated as a specialized skill, not a simple loading checklist.

Worked example

A container ship calling at three ports needs to load boxes for the second and third ports during the same visit to the first port. The stowage plan places third-port cargo deepest in the hold, second-port cargo above it, and leaves the top tiers open for cargo that will actually load at the second port — ensuring that when the ship reaches the second port, the boxes due off there are accessible without shifting anything destined for the third port.

Teaching note

Learners often assume stability is only about weight amount, not weight placement. Emphasize placement: the exact same total cargo weight, stowed differently, can be either perfectly safe or dangerously unstable.

Dangerous Goods & the IMDG Code

≈ 30 min

Cargo classified as dangerous — explosives, flammable liquids and gases, corrosives, oxidizers, and several other hazard classes — is governed internationally by the International Maritime Dangerous Goods (IMDG) Code, which sets classification, packaging, labeling, and documentation requirements so that everyone handling a shipment, anywhere in the world, understands exactly what it is and how it must be treated.

A core IMDG concept is segregation: certain hazard classes must be stowed a minimum distance apart, or on entirely separate decks, because they could react dangerously if they came into contact or were involved in the same fire or spill. A shipper must declare dangerous cargo accurately on a dangerous goods declaration, and a ship's officers cross-check that declaration against the stowage plan before agreeing to load it — misdeclared dangerous cargo is a well-documented cause of major shipboard fires, a topic Lesson 11 examines as a case study.

Anchoring analogy

IMDG segregation rules work like keeping cleaning chemicals that react dangerously together on separate shelves at home \u2014 except at ship scale, "separate shelves" can mean separate decks, separate holds, or a fire-rated bulkhead between two classes of cargo that must never end up next to each other.

The Bill of Lading

≈ 30 min

A bill of lading (B/L) is the single most important document in cargo shipping, and it does three distinct jobs at once. First, it is a receipt: proof that the carrier actually received the cargo described, in the condition stated. Second, it is evidence of the contract of carriage, referencing the terms under which the cargo is being carried. Third — and most distinctively — it is a document of title: whoever legitimately holds the original bill of lading has the legal right to claim the cargo at its destination.

That third function is why a bill of lading is often described as a negotiable document: it can be bought, sold, or used as collateral while the cargo is still at sea, because possessing the document is treated as equivalent to possessing the cargo itself for legal and financing purposes. This is also why a carrier will not release cargo to anyone who cannot present a valid original bill of lading, even the cargo's original owner, if that owner has since sold the goods to someone else by transferring the document.

Worked example

A coffee exporter in Colombia ships 20 containers to a roaster in Germany and receives a bill of lading naming the roaster as consignee. While the ship is still at sea, the roaster resells the coffee to a distributor by endorsing and transferring the bill of lading. When the ship arrives, the carrier releases the cargo to the distributor — who was never the original buyer — because they hold the valid, properly transferred bill of lading, and that document, not the original sales contract, controls who can claim the goods.

Special Cargo Considerations

≈ 15 min

Some cargo types need handling beyond a standard load/discharge sequence. Heavy-lift or project cargo — turbines, prefabricated modules, transformers — often exceeds a terminal's normal crane capacity and requires specialized heavy-lift vessels or shore cranes, plus detailed lifting and securing plans. Refrigerated containers (reefers) carry temperature-sensitive cargo like produce or pharmaceuticals and must be plugged into the ship's power supply and continuously monitored, since a lost power connection can spoil an entire container's contents within hours.

Deck cargo — containers or equipment carried on the open deck rather than below — must be lashed and secured against the ship's motion in open seas, and is more exposed to weather damage and, in extreme conditions, loss overboard than cargo stowed below deck. Each of these special cases is really the same lesson from Section 2 applied more strictly: cargo needs the right plan, not just the right hold.
