Safety Culture & Incident Types

Groundings, collisions, fires, and pollution incidents rarely have one cause. This lesson reads real and composite case studies through the lens of how safety actually fails — and how it's rebuilt afterward.

Lesson Overview

Every convention in Lesson 8, and much of the construction detail in Lesson 6, exists because of an incident. This lesson looks directly at the major categories of physical and operational maritime incident — grounding, collision, fire, and pollution — not to dwell on tragedy, but to understand the pattern investigators keep finding: serious incidents are almost never caused by one single failure. They happen when several smaller, individually survivable problems line up at the same time.

This lesson is explicitly about physical and operational safety, not cybersecurity — that subject, and how it fits alongside these same incident-causation ideas, is exactly where the companion Maritime OT Security course (previewed in Lesson 12) picks up.

Anchoring analogy

Imagine several slices of Swiss cheese stacked together, each slice representing one layer of defense — a rule, a piece of equipment, a trained habit. Each slice has holes (weaknesses) in different places. Most of the time, a hole in one slice is blocked by solid cheese in the next. An incident happens on the rare occasion when the holes in every single slice happen to line up at once, letting a hazard pass straight through every layer of defense. This is the "Swiss cheese model" investigators use to explain why serious incidents are almost always multi-causal.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what safety culture means beyond simple rule compliance.
  • Describe the Swiss cheese (multi-layered defense) model of incident causation.
  • Identify the defining features of grounding, fire, and pollution incidents through case studies.
  • Explain the role of near-miss reporting and a "just culture" in preventing incidents.
  • Explain how incident investigation findings have historically fed back into regulation and design.

What Is Safety Culture?

≈ 25 min

Safety culture is the set of shared values, attitudes, and everyday behaviors around risk — distinct from simply having safety rules and equipment aboard. A ship can technically comply with every SOLAS and ISM Code requirement from Lesson 8 and still have a weak safety culture, if crew feel unable to report problems, if shortcuts are quietly tolerated to save time, or if raising a concern is seen as complaining rather than professionalism.

A genuine safety culture empowers anyone aboard — not only the master invoking the overriding authority from Lesson 4 — to pause an unsafe activity without fear of blame, on the understanding that a false alarm costs far less than a missed one. This is the practical, day-to-day expression of everything the conventions in Lesson 8 are trying to achieve on paper.

Teaching note

Ask learners for a non-maritime example of "rules followed, but culture weak" from their own experience (a workplace, a lab, a kitchen) \u2014 this concept transfers easily once anchored to something familiar.

The Incident Causation Model

≈ 25 min

The Swiss cheese model, developed by psychologist James Reason, frames serious incidents as the alignment of weaknesses across multiple independent layers of defense — procedures, training, equipment, supervision — rather than the result of one person's single mistake. Investigators use this model specifically to resist the temptation to stop investigating once they've found one plausible cause; a thorough investigation keeps asking what else had to also go wrong for that one factor to result in an actual incident.

This model reframes the case studies in the rest of this lesson: each one involves a specific triggering event, but each also involves several contributing factors — a maintenance decision, a communication gap, a design limitation, a weather condition — none of which alone would likely have caused the outcome. That reframing is deliberate, and it's the same reasoning this course applies throughout: understanding a system, whether it's the shipping industry's ownership structure or an incident's causes, means resisting single-factor explanations.

Case Study: Grounding — the 2021 Suez Canal Blockage

≈ 35 min

In March 2021, the ultra-large container ship Ever Given grounded diagonally across the Suez Canal, blocking one of the world's busiest shipping chokepoints for roughly six days and disrupting a substantial share of global east-west trade until the vessel was refloated. Investigations and public reporting pointed to a combination of contributing factors rather than one single cause: strong winds and a sandstorm affecting the very large hull's windage, hydrodynamic effects related to a large ship transiting a narrow, shallow channel (sometimes described as bank effect and squat), and questions raised about speed and passage planning through that specific stretch of canal.

The incident is a vivid illustration of two ideas from earlier lessons at once: Lesson 1's point about ship size (the largest container ships bring real handling challenges specific to their scale) and Lesson 2's point about global trade concentration (a huge share of world trade routes through a small number of chokepoints, so a single grounding can ripple through supply chains worldwide within days).

Worked example

Analysts estimated that the six-day blockage held up billions of dollars of trade per day at its peak, with knock-on delays affecting shipping schedules, freight rates, and connected cargo (some of it perishable or time-sensitive) for weeks afterward — a scale of consequence entirely disproportionate to a single vessel's grounding, precisely because of how much global trade funnels through that one canal.

Case Study: Collision — a Composite Crossing-Situation Scenario

≈ 30 min

Rather than a single named incident, this section uses a composite scenario built from patterns that recur across real collision investigations, because the underlying causal pattern matters more here than any one vessel's name: two vessels converge in a crossing situation (the COLREGs scenario from Lesson 4). The give-way vessel's watch officer, alone on a busy bridge without a dedicated lookout, is absorbed in a routine administrative task and doesn't notice the developing risk of collision in time. The stand-on vessel, per COLREGs, maintains course and speed, correctly expecting the other vessel to act — until it becomes clear that it isn't going to, at which point avoiding action is taken too late to prevent contact.

Real investigations into collisions like this one consistently point to the same recurring contributing factors: an inadequate or absent dedicated lookout, over-reliance on radar/ARPA without proper visual cross-checks, fatigue, and a bridge team that functioned as one overloaded individual rather than the team model from Lesson 4. None of these factors alone typically causes a collision — together, they are the Swiss cheese holes lining up.

Anchoring analogy

This scenario is exactly what Lesson 4's bridge-workload simulation modeled: stack enough concurrent duties onto one watch officer, remove the dedicated lookout, and the probability of missing a developing risk climbs sharply \u2014 not because any one person was careless, but because the workload design left too little margin for error.

Case Study: Fire & Pollution, and Feeding Findings Back into Regulation

≈ 30 min

In May 2021, the container ship X-Press Pearl caught fire off the coast of Sri Lanka while carrying, among other cargo, nitric acid and other chemicals; a leak had reportedly been identified in port calls earlier in the voyage, but the vessel was not able to offload the affected cargo before the fire developed at sea. The vessel burned for days and eventually sank, releasing cargo debris — including a large quantity of plastic resin pellets — that caused one of the more significant marine pollution incidents in the region's recent history, affecting beaches and marine life along the Sri Lankan coastline.

This case connects directly back to Lesson 5's dangerous goods and IMDG content: accurate declaration, proper segregation, and a timely response to a known leak are exactly the safeguards that section described, and gaps in any of them are widely discussed as contributing factors in this incident. It also illustrates why near-miss reporting matters — an earlier, non-catastrophic leak being taken seriously and acted on decisively is precisely the kind of intervention a strong safety culture and a just culture (one that distinguishes honest error deserving support from genuine recklessness deserving accountability) is designed to encourage, rather than being downplayed to avoid delay or blame.

Every major incident this lesson has covered eventually fed back into the regulatory system from Lessons 7 and 8: the Titanic into SOLAS, the Exxon Valdez into MARPOL's double-hull requirement, the Herald of Free Enterprise into the ISM Code. Investigations by flag states and international bodies under the IMO's Casualty Investigation Code exist specifically to close this loop — turning a specific incident's contributing factors into a durable, generalized rule that (ideally) prevents the same alignment of failures from recurring elsewhere.

Teaching note

Close this lesson by asking learners to predict, out loud, what a plausible regulatory or design response to a documented gap in dangerous-goods leak escalation procedures might look like \u2014 this rehearses the historical pattern just described and previews the forward-looking, generative thinking Lesson 12's capstone will ask of them.
