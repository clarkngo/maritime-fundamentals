Prerequisite Check

There isn't one — that's the defining design choice of this course. Unlike most maritime training, which assumes some existing industry exposure, MOF-101 is built for learners with zero shipping background: a career-changer, a student, a shore-based hire at a logistics company, or anyone preparing for Maritime OT Security who needs the industry context that course assumes.

No maritime vocabulary assumed

Every technical term is introduced with a plain-language definition and an anchoring analogy before it's used again.

No mathematics beyond basic arithmetic

Stability, freight rates, and laytime calculations (Lessons 5, 6, and 10) are explained conceptually, not computed with formulas.

No prior regulatory knowledge assumed

Lessons 7 and 8 build the entire regulatory ecosystem from zero, rather than assuming familiarity with any convention by name.

Common Misconceptions

Newcomers consistently arrive with a few specific wrong mental models. Naming these directly and early heads off confusion later.

"The shipping company does everything."

Lesson 2 exists specifically to break this apart into owner, manager, and operator/charterer — three usually-separate companies.

"All cargo ships are basically the same."

Lesson 1's vessel-type tour and specialization-trade-off simulation directly counters this.

"The captain personally handles all navigation."

Lesson 4's bridge team content corrects the lone-hero image many learners bring from film and television.

"Shipping is a low-tech, old-fashioned industry."

Lessons 4 and 6 (ECDIS, AIS, engine automation) and Lesson 1's containerization history push back on this directly.

"A pilot takes over command of the ship."

Lesson 4 explicitly corrects this — the master retains command throughout pilotage.

"Classification is the same as insurance."

Lesson 7 and the Classification Societies reference page draw this distinction explicitly and repeatedly.

Discussion Prompts

Day 1 Prompt

If you had to design a new vessel type for a cargo that doesn't exist yet, what would it need to carry, and which existing vessel type (Lesson 1) would it most resemble?

Day 2 Prompt

Lesson 4's bridge team model and Lesson 5's stowage planning both stack specialized roles or plans on top of a single ship. Where else in daily life do you see a similar layering of specialized roles managing one shared, high-stakes system?

Day 3 Prompt

Lesson 7 presents five separate oversight bodies as a deliberate design, not an accident. Can you think of another industry that spreads oversight across multiple independent bodies this way, and does it work as well?

Day 4 Prompt

Lesson 9's fixed-term contract and rotation model is unusual compared to most shore-based careers. What do you think would be the hardest personal adjustment about seafaring work, and what part of Lesson 9's welfare content addresses it?

Day 5 Prompt

Lesson 11's Swiss cheese model insists that incidents are multi-causal. Can you think of a non-maritime incident (a data breach, a building fire, a recall) you know about, and identify at least two independent contributing factors behind it?

Facilitation Tips

  1. 1

    Use the anchoring analogies deliberately. Every lesson's analogy box is designed to be referenced again later in the same lesson or a subsequent one — actively calling back to it ("remember the apartment-building analogy from Lesson 2?") reinforces retention more than introducing a new one each time.

  2. 2

    Let the simulations run twice. Have learners predict the outcome before toggling each factor in a lesson's simulation, then run it and compare — prediction-then-check builds stronger recall than passive interaction alone.

  3. 3

    Reuse the mapping-activity format for your own quick checks. The five-row mapping table pattern used in every lesson is easy to improvise live as a whiteboard exercise if you want an additional low-stakes check partway through a lesson.

  4. 4

    Bridge explicitly to MOT-101 more than once. Don't save the connection to Maritime OT Security for Lesson 12 alone — Lessons 4, 6, and 8 all contain natural, brief moments to mention it for learners who are already curious.

7

Adapting for Self-Paced Delivery

Every mechanic in this course — theme toggle, progress tracker, mapping activity, simulation, quiz — works identically for a single self-paced learner as it does in a facilitated cohort.

Pacing guidance

A self-paced learner can reasonably complete one lesson per sitting (roughly 2-3 hours including all activities), finishing the course in two to three weeks at a relaxed pace.

Discussion prompts as journaling

Section 4's prompts work well as private written reflection for a self-paced learner without a cohort to discuss them with.

Capstone teaching substitute

A self-paced learner without a peer audience for Lesson 12 can instead write out their module as if presenting it, or record a short explanation aloud, to preserve the retrieval-practice benefit of teaching.
