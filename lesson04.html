Basic Navigation & Watchkeeping

How a ship's bridge stays safely aware of its position and surroundings, twenty-four hours a day, on every day of a voyage.

📊 Beginner ⏱ 3 hours 🎯 5 objectives
Overview The Bridge Team Watch Schedules COLREGs Basics Voyage Planning Pilotage Activity Simulation Review

Lesson Overview

A ship never stops moving, and it can never simply pull over. That single fact shapes almost everything about how a bridge is staffed and run: someone qualified must be watching the ship's position, its surroundings, and its systems at every hour of every day, for the entire length of a voyage that might last weeks. This lesson introduces the people who do that watching, the schedule that keeps them fresh enough to do it safely, the basic rules that govern how ships behave around each other, and the planning process that turns a destination into an actual route.

None of this lesson requires a navigation background. The goal is industry literacy: knowing who is on the bridge, what they're responsible for, and roughly how a ship gets from one port to another safely — not how to actually navigate a vessel yourself.

Anchoring analogy

Think of an aircraft's cockpit crew, but for a much longer, slower journey: instead of a few hours in the air, a bridge team maintains this same kind of constant vigilance for the entire span of a multi-day or multi-week ocean crossing, handing off responsibility from one qualified officer to the next in scheduled shifts rather than ever leaving the "cockpit" unattended.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Identify the core bridge team roles and describe the master's overall authority and responsibility.
  • Explain why continuous watchkeeping is required and how watch schedules are typically structured.
  • Describe the basic purpose of COLREGs and the concept of give-way and stand-on vessels.
  • Explain what voyage planning involves at a high level, from appraisal to monitoring.
  • Explain the role of a maritime pilot during port approaches and restricted-water transits.

The Bridge Team

≈ 30 min

The master holds overall command of the vessel and ultimate responsibility for its safety, the safety of everyone aboard, and the cargo — a legal and practical authority sometimes called the master's overriding authority, meaning that in a genuine safety emergency, the master's judgment on the spot outranks any commercial pressure, schedule, or shore-side instruction. Below the master, an officer of the watch (OOW) — a licensed deck officer — takes direct responsibility for the ship's safe navigation during their assigned watch period, supported by a lookout (who watches visually and by ear for hazards) and, when the ship is being steered manually, a helmsman at the wheel.

This is a chain of delegated, not divided, responsibility: the OOW runs the watch, but the master remains ultimately accountable and can be called to the bridge at any time — particularly for complex maneuvers, restricted visibility, or congested waters — without that call reflecting badly on the watch officer. Calling the master early is the expected, professional choice, not a last resort.

Teaching note

Learners sometimes assume calling the captain signals failure on the watch officer's part. Correct this directly: maritime safety culture treats early escalation as good practice, a theme Lesson 11 returns to in the context of incident causation.

Watch Schedules & Continuous Watchkeeping

≈ 25 min

Because a vessel operates continuously, its bridge (and engine room) must be staffed around the clock, divided into watch systems — commonly a 4-on/8-off rotation, where each watchkeeper stands four hours on duty followed by eight hours off, repeated twice daily. Smaller crews sometimes use a 6-on/6-off system instead. Either way, the goal is the same: guarantee that a rested, qualified officer is always in charge of the watch.

International rules under the STCW Convention (introduced fully in Lesson 8) set minimum rest-hour requirements specifically to prevent fatigue-driven errors, because a tired watch officer is a well-documented contributing factor in maritime incidents. Watch schedules are not just a staffing convenience — they are a core safety control, and disrupting them (for example, by demanding extra work during off-watch rest periods) is treated as a genuine safety risk, not just an inconvenience.

Worked example

A cargo ship crossing the Pacific runs a 4-on/8-off watch system with three deck officers. The second officer stands the 12:00–16:00 and 00:00–04:00 watches every day of the voyage, handing over a written and verbal briefing to the third officer at each changeover — position, traffic, weather, and any standing orders from the master — so that the ship's situational awareness never has a gap, even as the specific person maintaining it changes every four hours.

COLREGs: The Basic Rules of the Road at Sea

≈ 35 min

The International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREGs) are the maritime equivalent of traffic rules — a globally agreed set of rules governing how vessels behave around each other to avoid collision. At the core of COLREGs is a simple but essential concept: in most encounters between two vessels, one is designated the give-way vessel, obligated to take early and substantial action to keep clear, while the other is the stand-on vessel, obligated to maintain course and speed so the give-way vessel can predict its movement and maneuver around it safely.

Which vessel gives way depends on the situation — head-on, crossing, or overtaking — and on vessel type and status (a sailing vessel or one restricted in its ability to maneuver generally has priority over an ordinary power-driven vessel). COLREGs also requires every vessel to maintain a proper lookout "by sight and hearing" at all times and to proceed at a safe speed appropriate to visibility, traffic density, and sea conditions — the same lookout duty that keeps the bridge team from Section 1 continuously staffed.

Anchoring analogy

COLREGs' give-way/stand-on system works like a four-way stop sign with an agreed pecking order: everyone needs to know, without radio contact and often at a distance of miles, exactly which vessel is expected to move and which is expected to hold steady \u2014 predictability is what actually prevents the collision, more than any single vessel's cleverness.

Voyage Planning

≈ 30 min

Before a ship departs, its officers develop a passage plan (or voyage plan) covering the entire route, following a widely used four-stage process: appraisal (gathering all relevant charts, weather, and hazard information for the intended route), planning (laying out the actual route and waypoints, including contingencies), execution (carrying out the plan during the voyage), and monitoring (continuously checking the ship's actual position and conditions against the plan, and adjusting as needed).

Modern voyage planning is done largely on ECDIS (the electronic chart system mentioned in Lesson 1 and covered again in Lesson 6), which lets officers draw a planned route directly onto an electronic chart, check it against known hazards and under-keel clearance requirements, and monitor the ship's actual track against that plan in real time throughout the voyage — while paper charts and manual methods remain a required backup skill.

The four stages of voyage planning
StageWhat Happens
AppraisalGather charts, weather, hazard data, and vessel-specific constraints for the intended route
PlanningLay out the route, waypoints, and contingency options in detail
ExecutionCarry out the plan during the actual voyage
MonitoringContinuously check actual position and conditions against the plan, adjusting as needed

Pilotage in Restricted Waters

≈ 20 min

A maritime pilot is a local navigation specialist with detailed knowledge of a specific port, river, or restricted channel, who boards a vessel — often via a rope pilot ladder lowered from the ship's side while both vessels are underway — to advise the master through the trickiest part of a voyage: the approach into port. Pilots are typically required at most major ports for vessels above a certain size, precisely because local currents, traffic patterns, and underwater hazards are things a visiting ship's own officers cannot be expected to know as well as someone who transits that same channel daily.

Critically, a pilot advises; the master retains full command and legal responsibility for the vessel throughout the pilotage, and can (rarely, but validly) override a pilot's recommendation if the master judges it unsafe. In practice, the pilot and bridge team function as an extended, temporary bridge team for the highest-risk phase of the voyage — the same collaborative model from Section 1, with one more highly specialized person added to it.

Teaching note

A common misconception is that the pilot "takes over" the ship. Correct this directly: legal command never transfers, which matters when the course later discusses accountability during incidents in Lesson 11.
